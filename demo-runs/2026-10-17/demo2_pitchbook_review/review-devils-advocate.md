# GraspLoop AI: devil's advocate review

**FICTIONAL COMPANY - TEACHING MATERIAL - ALL COMPANY DATA INVENTED**

Review date: 2026-10-17, America/Los_Angeles. Company data as of 2026-09; supplied monthly series spans 2025-09 to 2026-09. Source deck: supplied 11-page `inputs/pitch-deck.pdf`; PDF page and displayed slide number agree, 1-11. Source data: `inputs/data-room/kpi-monthly.csv` and its README. CSV physical row numbers below include the header as row 1. No external verification was attempted or needed for this fictional exercise. Supplied reference outputs were not used.

## Recommendation

**Pass on approving the proposed investment now; continue targeted diligence conditionally.** The strongest objection is that the current deck does not let an investor underwrite repeatable paid deployment economics or the cash required to scale. This is a critique of the supplied presentation and evidence, not a judgment about founder intent. Some claims conflict with the supplied KPI definitions; many other claims are simply unverifiable with this data room. Improving fleet metrics and actual recurring revenue merit further questions.

## Five strongest reasons to pass, ranked

### 1. Traction combines unlike commercial and operating measures

Page 6 presents $2.4M ARR, 14 customers, 99.7% pick success and 160% NRR without the qualifying definitions needed to interpret them. September 2026 (CSV row 14) has $161,000 paying MRR, or **$1.932M paying ARR**, alongside 12 unpaid pilot robots. The supplied booked figure is $2.436M, including $504,000 annualized pilot revenue that is not paying recurring revenue under the bundled rubric. The customer count reconciles to **11 paying + 3 unpaid pilot customers**; it should not be read as 14 paying customers. The identities and commercial status of the claimed Fortune 500 logos are unavailable.

The 99.7% result is the **best-site, single-SKU** value; the mixed-SKU fleet value is **97.2%** in the same month. That is a scope mismatch, not proof that the 99.7% measurement never occurred. Reported NRR comes from the three-customer September 2025 cohort, not an established broad customer base: $38,500 / $24,500 = **157.14%** (rows 2 and 14), reasonably near a coarsely rounded 160%, but its small cohort must be disclosed. These labels change how much confidence an investor can place in repeatability.

**Required repair:** separate paid revenue, unpaid pilots, and booked/potential revenue; state metric populations and cohort size; reconcile logo status to contracts.

### 2. Observed economics do not support the headline economics as actuals

Page 7 states **68% gross margin** and **4-month CAC payback**, with no target/actual distinction or calculation inputs. September actual gross margin in row 14 is **41%**. That is a material difference. The supplied data cannot establish whether 68% is an aspiration, a special deployment, or a separately defined metric.

Using the bundled aggregate formula with Q3 sales and marketing, net new MRR, and September margin gives **$490,000 / [($161,000 - $115,500) x 41%] = 26.27 months** (Q3 spend rows 12-14; beginning MRR row 11). This is the interval that supports an approximately 26-month teaching result. **The actual deck, page 7, claims 4 months; it does not claim 26 months.** September-only produces approximately **29.62 months**; the trailing 12-month proxy approximately **27.87 months**, both at September margin. A point-in-time margin applied across a period is a methodological choice.

These are net-new-MRR proxies, not identified acquisition-cohort payback: expansion, contraction, churn, spending lag, and customer acquisition attribution are absent. Thus the calculation challenges the unsupported four-month presentation but does not prove exact true cohort CAC is 26.27 months. Page 7's nine-month sales cycle also lacks contract-level evidence; it suggests lag is worth testing, not an algebraic contradiction by itself.

**Required repair:** show the acquisition cohort, spend period, lag, gross profit, and hardware treatment behind actual payback; label targets separately.

### 3. The raise lacks a funded scale plan

Pages 10-11 project $100M ARR by 2029 and request $18M with headcount increasing from 38 to 85 and 24 months of runway. No hiring dates, compensation, deployment schedule, working-capital assumptions, capex schedule, or equipment financing terms are supplied. The KPI series cannot verify that forward plan.

September cash **$6,038,215** divided by current monthly burn **$713,990** is **8.46 months before the raise**. Adding the proposed $18M at unchanged burn gives **33.67 months**. Neither proves or disproves 24 months after the raise. If the entire $18M is received immediately and there are no fees or other cash adjustments, 24 months permits average all-in net cash use of approximately **$1.0016M per month**. The plan must fit within that budget, including any capital spending and working-capital needs. These are conditional static calculations, not forecasts.

Page 7 gives $42,000 hardware cost per robot; that cost itself is unverified by invoices or a bill of materials. The CSV shows no separate capex line, but this does **not** establish that hardware cash flows were omitted rather than embedded in other categories. Deployment hardware estimates would therefore be scenarios, conditional on ownership, financing, and cost assumptions. Page 10 also starts from the $2.4M figure that includes pilots, compounding the need for a paying-revenue bridge.

**Required repair:** reconcile opening cash and net raise proceeds to a monthly hiring, deployment, margin, and cash-flow plan, with downside cases and milestone gates.

### 4. The learning loop is plausible but defensibility is unproven

Page 3 describes an off-the-shelf arm, proprietary gripper and model, and remote operators whose fixes become training data. Page 8 positions GraspLoop above unnamed competitive categories. This is a coherent mechanism, but no controlled learning evaluation, performance distribution, rights to customer data, model licensing, switching costs, patent evidence, or named competitor comparison is supplied. Claims of installation in one day and learning a SKU in ten minutes from 20 picks cannot be verified from monthly totals.

The fictional data does show improvement: mixed-SKU fleet success rises **94.1% to 97.2%**, while interventions decline **41 to 14 per 1,000 picks** (rows 2 and 14). The appropriate inference is that reported aggregate performance improved. Changes in sites, SKU mix, interventions, or customer selection could also affect results. These aggregates do not isolate LoopBrain's causal contribution or demonstrate a durable moat. There is no basis to claim that the moat is rented or that IP is owned by someone else.

**Required repair:** present a fixed-site/SKU or controlled evaluation, intervention and cost distributions, and documented rights to the data/model improvement loop; compare real alternatives on the same workload.

### 5. The obtainable market and competitive win case are not substantiated

Page 5 states $120B TAM, $9B SAM, and $450M SOM without sources, customer counts, rollout assumptions, or a pricing bridge. Page 8 compares broad categories rather than named competitors, cost structures, and procurement alternatives. Page 2's labor and cost-per-pick statistics and page 4's timing claims have no corroborating inputs. These are **unverifiable in the supplied material**, not established falsehoods.

The investor cannot connect the target segment's sites and robots per site to a credible pace of installation and paid adoption, particularly alongside the page 10 growth projection. A large market label is insufficient evidence that this company can win its proposed share at attractive economics. This objection ranks below the directly evidenced metric and economic issues.

**Required repair:** build a bottom-up segment and deployment model, distinguish total market from reachable customers, and show win/loss or customer comparison evidence against specific alternatives.

## Fairness and prioritization

Named relevant roles on page 9 merit credit as a team presentation, although affiliations, exits, and operational claims need references. I agree with the narrative reviewer that Team presentation 3/5 is defensible and Market & competition 2/5 fits the supplied rubric. Vague bios are not in my top five reasons to pass, and missing detail is not evidence of a nonexistent team or market. This review does not independently authenticate any fictional company claim.
